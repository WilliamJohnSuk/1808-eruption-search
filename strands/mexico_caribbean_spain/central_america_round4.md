# Central America and southern Mexico as the 1808/09 source (round 4, 16 Oct 2026)

Thread: "Central American volcanoes" (William asked whether Central American volcanoes could be implicated as some of the closest to Bogotá, then asked to check wind directions and where the haze could have blown in from). Builds on notes/mexico_centralam_round3.md, notes/spanish_north.md, notes/gvp_1806_1811.md and notes/caribbean_papers_round3.md; it does not repeat their searches.

## Verdict
- **Physically possible, documentarily and geologically unsupported.** Central American and Chiapas volcanoes (10–17°N) are tropical enough for a bipolar sulfate signal, and their magmas are sulfur-rich. But an eruption big enough for 1809 (≈19 Tg S, about twice Pinatubo) would have been the largest event in the region between El Chichón ~1360 and Santa María 1902, and it has left no deposit, no catalogue entry and no mention in Juarros or Montessus. The one regional analogue we can calibrate against, Cosigüina 1835, was heard and dropped ash at Bogotá; Caldas in 1808 described only a transparent veil.
- **"Closest" is not the right test.** Bogotá–Central America is 1,200–2,200 km (Irazú 1,232; Cosigüina 1,750; Izalco 1,989; Fuego 2,142; Santa María 2,221; El Chichón 2,522; Colima 3,604 km great-circle, computed here). That is about the same as Bogotá–Lima (1,880 km). Colombian and Ecuadorian volcanoes are far closer. More importantly, a stratospheric veil carries almost no information on where it came from (see Winds).

## 2. Ice-core tephra chemistry
- Antarctic 1809 glass (Yalcin et al. 2006 table, from Palais et al. 1990 and De Angelis et al. 1985; https://scholars.unh.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?httpsredir=1&article=1538&context=earthsci_facpub, text):
  - South Pole (n=8): SiO2 60.28, TiO2 0.84, FeO 7.91, MgO 2.35, CaO 5.45, Na2O 4.26, K2O 3.11 (sd 1.38).
  - Dome C (n=13): SiO2 59.44, TiO2 0.67, FeO 9.00, MgO 2.83, CaO 5.44, Na2O 4.14, K2O 2.05.
  - Andesite to trachyandesite; the two sets share a source. Eclipse (Yukon) is a different, trachytic/dacitic source (SiO2 64.9, K2O 2.70) with Alaskan/Aleutian/Kamchatkan matches suggested.
- **A Central American source was proposed once.** Yalcin et al.: Palais et al. (1990) proposed Cosigüina as the source of the South Pole/Dome C 1809 tephra, on chemical similarity to Cosigüina's 1835 products and the GVP "March 1809" date; Yalcin calls this "no longer supported" because Scott et al. 2006 found no 1809 deposit. Palais et al. 1990 abstract itself (Ann. Glaciol. 14:216–220, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annals-of-glaciology/article/identification-of-some-global-volcanic-horizons-by-major-element-analysis-of-fine-ash-in-antarctic-ice/E1C240F1DD3064BF6EB0BD87D63D5361) speaks of shards "of global (Indonesian/South American) importance"; the 1809 table is an image and was not read here. Glass shards were <5 µm.
- **My comparison (inference, not a published result):** Cosigüina 1835 and 1709 rocks are basaltic andesite to andesite, SiO2 55.5–58.5 and K2O 0.97–1.44 (Hradecký & Rapprich 2008 Table 2, rock samples). The Antarctic glass has K2O 2–3 at ~60% SiO2, which is high-K. Matrix glass is more evolved than whole rock, so some K enrichment is expected, but 2–3% is high for the low-K Nicaraguan front. Guatemalan and Salvadoran fronts are medium-K; high-K glass of this kind is more typical of behind-arc Chiapas/Guatemala centres (El Chichón, Tacaná) and of Indonesia. With n=8–13 tiny shards and sd up to 1.4% K2O, this is weak evidence, not a discriminator.
- Siple Dome 1809/1810 trachyte (SiO2 63.4, K2O 4.91) is assigned to the Pleiades, Antarctica (Kurbatov et al. 2006, https://geoinfo.nmt.edu/staff/dunbar/publications/documents/kurbatov_ziel_dunbar_siple.pdf). The same paper does match other layers to Central American and Mexican sources (Tacaná, Ilopango, El Chichón, Fuego 1974), so Central American glass is reaching Antarctic cores and can be recognised.
- No trace-element or modern microprobe data on the 1809 Antarctic shards were found. That is the measurement that would settle it (Central American glass has distinctive high Ba/La and low Nb).

## 3. Sulfur yield and latitude
- eVolv2k: 19.3 Tg S for 1809 (IVI2 13.5), Tambora 28.1; Pinatubo ≈9 ± 2 Tg S (Toohey & Sigl 2017, ESSD 9:809, Table 2, text). 1835 Cosigüina is not in eVolv2k's 20 largest injections; 1809 is.
- eVolv2k assigns unidentified bipolar events to 0° by convention; no latitude can be read from it. Guevara-Murua et al. 2014 give Greenland 61% and Antarctica 45% of Tambora, which leans NH and fits a northern-tropical source, but the CMIP7 preprint's asymmetry formula has ±40° uncertainty, so this does not favour Central America over, say, the Philippines.
- Latitude is no obstacle: Cosigüina 1835 (13°N) and El Chichón 1982 (17°N) are both found in Antarctic cores (Ammann et al. 2003 list, https://paleo.ltrr.arizona.edu/climate_forcing/volcanic_aerosols/ammann2003_eruptions.pdf).
- Scale: 19 Tg S ≈ 2× Pinatubo, ≈5× El Chichón 1982 (≈3.5 Tg S). Even with S-rich Central American magma this means a large VEI 5 or a VEI 6.

## 4. Winds and where the haze could have come from
- **The veil was stratospheric** (Δ33S anomaly, Cole-Dai et al. 2009). In the tropical stratosphere the winds are mainly east–west and fast: standard figures (not re-checked here) are Krakatau 1883 circling the globe westward in about two weeks and Pinatubo 1991 in about three. Guevara-Murua et al. note Pinatubo and El Chichón gave a continuous 10°N–20°S layer within about two weeks. So within a few weeks a veil over Bogotá has lost the longitude of its source. Its arrival from "nearby" is not implied.
- Which way the stratospheric wind blew in Dec 1808 depends on the QBO phase, which is not known for 1808. Easterly phase carries clouds westward (Krakatau); westerly phase eastward. Either way an eruption anywhere in the tropics a few weeks before 11 Dec reaches Bogotá and Lima.
- **Unanue's glow "towards the SW" is the sunset direction, not a source direction.** Computed sunset azimuth in mid-December: Lima 246°, Bogotá 247° (WSW). A twilight glow always sits over the setting sun.
- **Lower atmosphere.** December–February: NE trade winds over the Caribbean and northern Colombia; upper-tropospheric westerlies over Central America. Cosigüina's January 1835 ash went NE to Jamaica (1,300 km), NW to Chiapas (600 km), SE to Costa Rica and as far as Bogotá, and booming was heard in Guatemala, Belize, Oaxaca, Jamaica and Bogotá (Scott et al. 2006). A bigger Central American eruption in Nov–Dec 1808 would therefore be expected in Jamaica, Belize and Caribbean Colombia first.
- **What Caldas did not see.** His correspondents covered Santa Marta, Pasto, Popayán, Neiva and Tunja. He reported a transparent, uniform veil, day and night, without ash or detonations. A near-field Central American plume would more likely have produced ashfall or noise at Santa Marta or Cartagena and an abrupt onset; a thin uniform veil is what a distant, already-spread stratospheric cloud looks like. Inference, but a strong one.
- The Barbados and Nassau papers Dec 1808 – Jun 1809 are negative (round 3); the Kingston *Royal Gazette*, the best English-language test because Jamaica heard and received ash from Cosigüina in 1835, is still unread.
