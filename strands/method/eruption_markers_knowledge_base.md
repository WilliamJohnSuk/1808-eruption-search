# Knowledge base: what large eruptions leave in written records

Written 21 Oct 2026 ("Eruption markers knowledge base" thread), first usable version. Purpose: describe what people near to and far from well-documented large eruptions actually wrote down, in their own words, so that the search-term list (notes/eruption_clue_search_terms.md) can be extended from evidence rather than guesswork. New terms derived from it are in that file under "Additions 21 Oct 2026".

**Verification levels used here** (project rule):
- **OCR only**: quoted from the archive.org full-text index of the named scan (inside-item search); spelling and page number as the OCR gives them; page image not looked at.
- **Secondary**: taken from a modern author's paraphrase or summary, not from the period text.
- **Background**: standard facts from the volcanological literature, stated from prior knowledge and not re-checked in this session. Use only as orientation.

## Part 1. Lessons for the 1808/09 search (the useful part)

1. **Distant explosions were almost never recognised as a volcano at first.** In every case read today, listeners named the most familiar loud noise of their own world:
   - artillery, a battle, an enemy fleet: Lima 1600 thought "los truenos que oíamos eran de la artillería que en la batalla se disparaban" (Cobo); Philippines 1641 heard "the noise in the air of musketry, artillery, and war drums" (Blair & Robertson 35);
   - a ship in distress or at gun practice: Krakatau 1883 sounds in Ceylon and the Indian Ocean "were supposed to be those of guns fired by a vessel in distress"; "practising with her big guns out of sight of land" (Royal Society report); Belize 1835 asked whether it was "a man-of-war in distress, or a naval action" (Galindo);
   - pirates: Makassar 1815 sent out a cruiser, "supposing it to be occasioned by pirates" (Raffles memoir p. 275);
   - troops marched out: Java 1815, "a detachment of troops were marched from Djocjocarta" (Raffles p. 271);
   - a local cause: on the Polochic 1835 Galindo suspected a drunken commandant firing guns at Izabal; Quetzaltenango blamed its own volcano; Arequipa 1600 thought for two days the ash came from a large volcano three leagues off (the nearer, wrong one).
   **Consequence for 1808-09:** wartime 1808-09 papers would file such reports as naval news, signals of distress, pirate alarms or "supposed engagements", and the local press would blame the nearest known volcano. Searching "volcano" alone misses them. Terms for distress guns, pirates, sea fights and troops turned out are added in every language.
2. **Distances.** Booms: Krakatau to Rodrigues, about 3,000 English miles (~4,800 km); Huaynaputina to Lima 164 leagues (~900 km); Cosegüina to Belize 350 miles and Jamaica 800 miles (heard), claimed at Bogotá (~1,800 km, hearsay); Tambora to Ternate (~1,400 km, "several very distinct reports like heavy cannon... in the S.W. quarter"). Ashfall: Krakatau dust on ships 900-1,100 miles away; Cosegüina ash on Jamaica ~700 miles; Huaynaputina "más de trescientas leguas". Darkness: tens to a few hundred km; lasted hours (Batavia 1883), 36-43 hours (Nicaragua 1835), "two days" (Arequipa 1600), and murk for 10-15 days.
3. **Sequence in time** (combine with notes/scientific_data_round6.md): booms and darkness the same day; pumice rafts at sea for months (Krakatau pumice reached Natal and Diego Garcia 13 months later, "thickly coated with barnacles"); coloured suns in the tropics within days to weeks; red twilights and the ring round the sun in the other hemisphere after 2-3 months; dry fog, pale sun and cold summer in the following year.
4. **What far-field writers called it** rarely includes the word "volcano": "peculiar haze, or smoky fog", "blank as a clouded moon", "rust-coloured ferruginous light" (White 1783); "nebbia secca... puzzolentissima" (Italy 1783); "sole... offuscato... i suoi raggi sembravano rossi" (Italy); "brouillard sec", "offuscation du soleil" (France); "blue and green suns", "silvery glare", "after-glow", "the moon... of a green colour" (1883). Ordinary people read the red sun as an omen ("superstitious awe", White 1783; "the day of judgement had arrived", Nicaragua 1835; "Divine anger", León 1835). So omen and sermon vocabulary is a search route too.
5. **Smells and falls of strange matter**: "a strong sulphurous smell" (ship near Krakatau); "hair" falling from the sky in Japan after Fuji 1707 (降毛); "white sand... like grains of mustard" (Arequipa 1600); mud rain on decks (Krakatau). Classical Chinese omen lists record "rain of hair" (天雨毛) and black rain: added as terms.
6. **Rivers and sea**: the Tambo river was dammed into a four-league lake and broke out; boiled fish piled on the shore (1600); the sea rose "nearly twelve feet" at Sanggar with floating trees (1815); masses of floating trees seen at sea after Tambora.

## Part 2. Reference eruptions

### Huaynaputina, Peru, Feb-Mar 1600 (VEI 6)
Source: Bernabé Cobo, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo* (written 1653), ed. Jiménez de la Espada, vol. 1, Sevilla 1890, ch. on volcanoes, pp. 212-224 of the scan. archive.org `historiadelnuev00cobogoog`. **OCR only.**
- Sound: "espantosos truenos á manera de artillería gruesa, tan de cerca" (p. 214); "los horribles truenos ó bramidos del volcán, los continuos y apresurados terremotos" (p. 219); "bramidos y temblores de tierra y lluvia de ceniza hacían guerra á un tiempo" (p. 215). Heard "en la ciudad de Lima, que está ciento y sesenta y cuatro leguas del volcán" (p. 220), where "los truenos que oíamos eran de la artillería que en la batalla se disparaban" (p. 220).
- Darkness: "era noche tan escura, que nadie conocía al que encontraba" (Arequipa, 2 p.m., p. 215); "no se vio en ellos el Sol" (two days, p. 216); darkness over "diez ó doce leguas de su contorno, que duraron quince días" (p. 217); "no se pudiese ver el Sol claro por muchos meses" (p. 218).
- Fall: "lo que de él llovió fué una arena blanca tan gruesa como granos de mostaza" (p. 215); "á llover ceniza desde las nueve hasta las tres de la tarde" (p. 216); ash covered the ground "una tercia en alto por más de cincuenta leguas" and spread "por más de trescientas leguas" (p. 221).
- Effects: "murieron todos los ganados y aves" (p. 221); Tambo river dammed, "hizo una laguna de cuatro leguas" (p. 222); fish ("lizas, pejereyes") heaped on the sea shore (p. 223); pumice carried to the sea (p. 223); raisin trade ruined (p. 224 note). Wrong attribution first: "pensaron los dos primeros días que salía de uno muy grande que estaba tres leguas" (p. 217).
- Earlier Quito 1586 in the same chapter: ash began at night and "no amaneció"; services held "con muchas luces de hachas y cirios"; the city vowed an annual commemoration (p. 212). Votive feasts and anniversaries are a further trace.

### Mindanao and northern Luzon, 4 Jan 1641 (Parker volcano and others)
Source: "News from Filipinas, 1640-42" (anonymous Franciscan, Manila), in Blair & Robertson, *The Philippine Islands 1493-1898*, vol. 35 (1906), English translation. archive.org `philippineisland35blai`. **OCR only.**
- Preface summary: "A terrible noise in the air has been heard throughout the islands" (p. 16).
- Text: "the noise in the air of musketry, artillery, and war drums was very distinctly [heard]" and "a loud noise from a volcano which was in a state of eruption in the kingdom of Mindanao" (p. 121). Fear was sharpened by news that the Dutch had taken Malacca (editors' preface, via Gutenberg text, **secondary**).
- Lesson: in a candidate region and a period of naval war, the first report was of an imagined battle in the air. Spanish originals would use "ruido de armas", "mosquetería", "artillería", "cajas" (war drums); added.

### Fuji (Hōei eruption), Japan, 16 Dec 1707
Sources: Koyama et al. 2004, "『伊能景利日記』と伊能景利採取標本", *Rekishi Jishin* 19: 38-46 (https://www.histeq.jp/kaishi/HE19/08-Koyama.pdf), quoting wrappers of ash and "hair" samples kept by Ino Kagetoshi of Sawara (Chiba), ~150 km from Fuji. **Secondary print of period wording (PDF text).** Also Itō Kazuaki, rodo.co.jp series no. 11 (**secondary**, modern paraphrase of Arai Hakuseki's *Oritaku shiba no ki* and Itō Sukeyoshi's diary).
- Wrapper: "宝永四年亥霜月廿三日昼過始テ當村降候冨士焼灰" (Fuji "burning ash" that began falling on this village past noon); "宝永四年亥霜月廿五日朝此邊降候毛" (hair that fell here); "天ヨリ降候毛" (hair fallen from heaven), into the following year.
- Paraphrased: darkness like night at midday in Edo; millet-sized black sand (黒砂) sounding on roofs like heavy rain; colds and coughing for a long time after; paper screens shaken on the first day by air waves.
- Terms: 焼灰, 降毛/毛降, 黒砂, 砂降, 障子 shaking, 咳/風邪 (added to Japanese).

### Tambora, Sumbawa, 5-11 Apr 1815 (VEI 7)
Source: Lady Sophia Raffles, *Memoir of the Life and Public Services of Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles* (London 1830), "Account of the eruption from the Tomboro mountain", printed pp. 241-250 (scan pp. 271-280). archive.org `memoiroflifepubl00raff`. **OCR only.**
- Sound: "almost universally attributed to distant cannon; so much so, that a detachment of troops were marched from Djocjocarta" (p. 271); at Makassar "Supposing it to be occasioned by pirates" (p. 275, the cruiser Benares sent out); at Ternate "several very distinct reports like heavy cannon were heard in the S.W. quarter" (p. 277).
- Darkness and fall: "the air became darkened by the quantity of falling ashes"; at Solo "objects were not visible at 300 yards distance" (p. 272); "the sun seemed shorn of its rays" (p. 272); "by four o'clock it was necessary to light candles" (p. 273); "By one, P.M. candles were necessary; by four, P.M. it was pitch-dark" (p. 274); "complete darkness covered the face of day"; sky "assumed a dusky red appearance" (p. 275); "By noon of the 12th, the sun made his appearance again, but very faintly" (p. 276).

- Far field (**background**, not re-checked): dry fog and red sun in the eastern USA spring 1816; sunspots seen with the naked eye through the haze; the "year without a summer" 1816 with June snows in New England, failed harvests, high grain prices and public prayers in Europe; famine in Yunnan 1815-17; a very dark lunar eclipse 10 Jun 1816 (Stothers 1984, Science 224; Stothers 2005 PASP is on William's Le Moyne list).

### Pinatubo, Philippines, 15 Jun 1991 (VEI 6). **Background only.**
The aerosol cloud circled the globe in about three weeks and covered the tropics within two months; vivid purple twilights and a Bishop's ring worldwide in the following months; the total lunar eclipse of 9 Dec 1992 was exceptionally dark; global mean cooling about 0.5 °C in 1992. ## Part 6. Blong 1982, chapter 13: what a few centimetres of ash does (added 22 Oct 2026)

Source: R. J. Blong, *The Time of Darkness* (1982), ch. 13 "The legends and reality: the effects of the tephra fall", pp. 158-173, and ch. 14 pp. 188-191. Read from William's screenshots (blong_pages p080-p087, p095-p096). **Page image verified** (of Blong's text; the eruption accounts he cites were not checked).

Blong compiled published effects of thin ash falls worldwide to test the Papua New Guinea legends. For our search, the useful point is that **2-10 cm of ash several hundred km from the vent leaves a whole set of farm and household marks that a local chronicle, missionary letter or Resident's report would record without ever using the word "volcano".**

- **Houses:** "tephra fall of only a few cm can cause roof collapse"; roof slope matters, and usually only a small share of buildings is affected (p. 160). Table 27: Tambora 1815, 9.5 cm, roofs collapsed 65 km away (Anon. 1816 p. 254); Soufrière St Vincent 1812, 1.5-2.5 cm, roofs collapsed; Vesuvius 1906, 2.5 cm, market roofs in Naples collapsed. "wet tephra is heavier than dry" (p. 159).
- **Crops and trees** (Table 28, p. 162): Tambora 3.2 cm "plants beaten down" 380 km away; 5 cm "crops somewhat injured" 740 km away; 20 cm "paddy totally destroyed" 400 km away (Raffles 1830). Cosegüina 10 cm, branches broken 100 km away. Soufrière 1902, 1.2 cm, "tender plants blighted and yellowed; breadfruit dropped"; 5 cm "crops burnt up, roasted by sand, forced ripening". Taal 1911, 1 cm, bananas killed. Mayon 1928: dust 10-15 km away made abaca leaves wilt and turn brown with black spots, from hydrochloric-acid films (p. 163). Leaves "perforated" by falling mud pellets (St Vincent 1902). Damage is often from acid coatings rather than the ash itself (p. 161).
- **Animals:** at Banyuwangi, 400 km west of Tambora, "126 horses and 86 cattle perished following a 20 cm tephra fall, 'chiefly for want of forage, during a month from the time of the eruption'" (Raffles 1830, cited p. 164). Birds killed, smothered or blinded, eyelids gummed with mud; cattle with bloodshot eyes and nasal infections; teeth worn by eating ash-covered grass; fluorine poisoning of sheep in Iceland (Laki 1783) (pp. 164-165).
- **Fish and water:** "only 3 cm of tephra killed many pond fish at Macassar, 400 km N of Tambora" (Anon. 1816 p. 253, cited p. 165); streams turbid for days and acid for hours (Mt Spurr 1953) (p. 166).
- **People:** the ash itself kills few; deaths come from roof collapse, tsunamis, pyroclastic flows and above all later starvation and disease, as on Sumbawa 1815 (p. 167).
- **Aftermath:** a grey, silent landscape and a short famine, then remarkably good harvests ("the best thing that ever happened to Kodiak"; Rabaul 1937) (pp. 168-169). A thin fall in the growing season does more harm than a thick one at another season (p. 161).
- **Caution on witnesses:** European eyewitness figures for ash thickness vary several-fold for the same place (Vesuvius 1906, Katmai 1912), so "European accounts should not be accepted uncritically" (p. 173). The legends overstated the darkness's duration in over 60% of cases but described its quality well (p. 170). Booms "could easily have been heard at distances of 500-600 km" from Long Island, and terrible smells also fit the historical accounts (pp. 170-171).

**What this adds to the search.** A local report of roofs falling in, paddy or gardens "burnt" or "scorched", leaves spotted or perforated, cattle and horses dying for want of fodder, pond fish dying, birds found dead or blinded, or water undrinkable for days, with no cause named, is a candidate ash-fall report up to 400-700 km from a vent. In 1808-09 this would sit in agricultural, mission or Residency reports, not in the shipping news. Terms added to the term file under "Additions 22 Oct 2026".

**Dating note for the oral-history strand (ch. 14, pp. 189-190).** Early lead-210 dates on lake sediments had put Tibito Tephra (Long Island) at 1805-1832 (Lake Egari) and 1815-1862 (Lake Ipea), and the c.r.s. model gave "just before 1820" and "just after 1800". Blong reports that Oldfield et al. (1980) withdrew these: corrected for radium in the tephra, they give "a revised age for Tibito Tephra of AD 1680-1690", and palaeomagnetic matching gives pre-1700. Blong himself prefers the radiocarbon dates (now 1651-1671, Blong et al. 2018). So **an early-19th-century date for the Long Island eruption is not supported**, despite the genealogies that point to 1803-1899 (see notes/oral_history_melanesia.md).

## Part 7. Blong 1982, the whole book: markers, numbers and leads (added 22 Oct 2026)

Source: Blong, *The Time of Darkness* (1982), chapters 2-15 and appendices 1-4, read from William's screenshots (blong_small/p005-p141). **Page image verified** for Blong's text; the eruption accounts he cites were not re-checked. Chapter-by-chapter notes are in notes/blong_time_of_darkness.md. Part 6 above covers chapter 13.

### 7.1 A Krakatau-sized eruption can go unrecorded in Melanesia
- Long Island (Bismarck Sea) erupted >11 km³ of airfall (>30 km³ in all), similar to Krakatau 1883, over ≥84,000 km² of New Guinea at >1.5 cm, in the 17th century, and "so far as is known no European navigators observed the eruption or its aftermath" (pp. 57-60, 68). Its only records are a tephra layer and oral legends.
- Bismarck arc records before 1870 are a few passing ships: Manam was seen in 1545, 1616, 1643, 1700 and (doubtfully) 1830, and Blong writes "Major eruptions, unreported in the European literature, could have occurred between 1616 and 1870" (pp. 41-42). Ritter erupted in 1700 and again in 1793, was quiet 1827-85, and McKee suggests near-continuous activity 1700-1888 (p. 43). Karkar: 1643, then 1885. **So a large 1808 eruption on the north New Guinea coast or in the Bismarck Sea would leave no European record unless a ship happened by.** (Inference.)
- Krakatau 1883 was not reported from New Guinea although it was surely heard there: "lack of communication would have prevented their association with the Krakatau event" (pp. 38-40).

### 7.2 Numbers for judging a distant report (from Blong's compilations)
- **Main eruptive phase** (Table 12, p. 60): Krakatau 1883 36-45 h; Tambora 1815 ~2 days; Cosegüina 1835 ~3 days; Katmai 1912 <20 h; Santa Maria 1902 ~18 h; Asama 1783 4 days. The Plinian phase is usually ≤48 h, often after months of minor activity. A far-field report of booms or darkness should therefore span one to three days, not weeks.
- **Ash-cloud speed** (Table 15, p. 65): St Vincent 1812, 160 km in 4.5 h (35 km/h); Cosegüina 1835, 1120 km in ~24 h (Caldcleugh 1836 p. 30); Sakurajima 1914, 960 km in 48 h; Quizapu 1932, 2960 km in 120 h; Askja 1875, 1800 km in 38 h. Usual range 25-100 km/h, 50-60 km/h common (p. 156). **Rule of thumb: ash arrives about a day per 1000 km after the bangs; sound arrives within hours.**
- **Darkness against ash thickness** (Table 24, pp. 143-144): about 4 mm of uncompacted ash per hour of darkness on average (range 0.7-23.5 mm/h). Examples: Tambora 1815 at 380 km, 3.2 cm and 19 h of total darkness; at 400-500 km, 72 h; Cosegüina 1835 at 80 km, 28 h of total darkness then 48 h of half-light (Caldcleugh 1836); Soufrière St Vincent 1812 at 160 km, 4.5 h total darkness then 7.5 h dim, 4 cm; Katmai 1912 at Kodiak, 160 km, 11-26 h, 12.5-25 cm. Thorarinsson: 0.5 cm of airborne ash gives total darkness. Fresh ash compacts to 0.4-0.6 of its first thickness within weeks (pp. 137-138).
- **Earthquakes felt from an eruption** (Table 25, p. 148): usually less than ~250 km away (Cosegüina 90 km; Tarawera 250 km; Shiveluch 430 km). Tambora 1815 "houses shook 780 km away in East Java" (Raffles 1830), possibly air waves.
- **Explosions heard** (Table 26, p. 149): Soufrière 1812, 1280 km (Heilprin 1903 p. 240); Tambora 1815, 1775 km; Cosegüina 1835, 430 km; Katmai 1912, 660-1440 km; Santa Maria 1902, 850 km; Krakatau 1883, 4930 km (Rodrigues). There is a "zone of silence": close in, a continuous roar; far off, distinct reports. Hekla 1947 was heard at 280 km but not in a belt about 70 km out (p. 152). Jet streams can amplify sound 15-fold (Bolt 1976).
- **How distant witnesses described the noise** (p. 151): "the continuous firing of heavy cannon" (Heilprin 1903 p. 239); "the explosion of dynamite in the nearby hills" (Erskine 1962 p. 52); "like someone banging an iron tank" (Pond & Smith 1886 p. 351). Cosegüina 1835: Colonel Galindo on the Río Polochic, about 300 km away, heard "noises like those of artillery" and supposed the commandant at Izabal was "in some sort of extraordinary inebriation" celebrating his birthday with gunfire (Williams 1952 p. 33). This matches the Part 3 lesson.
- **Smell and acid** (pp. 152-153): Makassar, 370 km from Tambora: the ash "was perfectly tasteless and did not affect the eyes ... had a faint burnt smell, but nothing like sulphur" (Anon. 1816 p. 253). Katmai 1912: at Kodiak (160 km) sulphur fumes made throats raw and rain drops "tasted like vinegar" and blistered lips; at Latouche (480 km) garden plants were destroyed and leaves burnt by acid rain (Griggs 1922 p. 25). Mayon 1928: leaves "burnt" by HCl-coated dust.
- **Temperature at the time of fall:** Katmai 1912, on the first night of ash fall the *Dora*'s crew "felt as though they were in the tropics" (Erskine 1962 p. 175). PNG legends say cold (six versions) as often as hot (three) (p. 154).
- **Tsunamis** (pp. 150-151): only at coasts and mostly with caldera collapse. Ritter 1888 sent tsunamis to the Huon coast, New Britain and Umboi. Miklouho-Maclay reports a massive Rai Coast tsunami about 1855-56 with many deaths (Sentinella 1975 p. 237), outside our window.
- **Optical effects** (p. 155): no PNG legend mentions sunsets or coloured suns, although Blong's questionnaire asked (Q. 36, "brilliant sunsets or blue moons"). He notes that afternoon cloud hides PNG sunsets. **So tropical silence on twilights is not evidence against an eruption.**

### 7.3 Legend wording (period-free phrases to search for in ethnographies, mission and patrol reports)
All are from Blong's quotations of informants (page image). Each is a pattern that an 1808-09 tradition elsewhere might share.
- Darkness: "the sun was not seen for many days, nor any light" (Manus, Carey 1938); "The sun and the moon vanished; they did not rise" (Enga 050, Meggitt 1973); "the sun and moon hid under the earth" (Waka-Enga 059); "the day did not lighten"; three days of darkness with light on the fourth (Agarabi; a folk pattern, Watson 1963); "a constant drift of black dirt fell from the cloud" for "three months" (Meggitt 1973 p. 54); "half light"; torches lit in daytime.
- What fell: "raining ashes" and an "ash-storm" (Melpa, Vicedom & Tischner 1948); "white pumiceous silt" (Huli *bingi*, Glasse 1963); sand on the thatch; "Skyfruits (the broken-up sky)" (Wanuma 094); "fell like marbles" (Waka-Enga 059); "The ground fell from the sky, not from the mountain" (Long Island); stones first, then ash (Wewak 099). Local words: Huli *bingi*, Duna *yu mo*, Enga *Yuu Kuia*, Dom *kilogebe* (dust or sand).
- Effects: houses fell or roofs were crushed; birds and cassowaries so loaded with ash they were caught by hand; a bird species vanished (Kaluli); streams choked, red or dammed by landslips (Meggitt p. 55); famine "after one month", "only a few people remained" (Melpa); "only three survivors" (Waka-Enga); a terrible smell "like rotten rubbish"; "the skin of the people turned black because of the dust" (Long Island).
- Dating hooks people attach to it: arrival of the sweet potato or of *Casuarina oligodon*; a bird or plant appearing or vanishing; a migration "since the time of darkness"; scabies afterwards; good harvests afterwards (Enga). The 1962 solar eclipse and 1950s airplanes were read as a return of the darkness.
- Look-alikes to discount: hailstorms and snow (eight accounts), eclipses (four), bush-fire smoke and frost (Mianmin, 1972), and Biblical Flood borrowings (pp. 82-87, 127, 147).
- Caution: genealogies telescope. The Long Island event, about 300 years old, was placed in the 1880s and dated 1803-1899 by genealogy, which is "seriously in error, many of them by 200 years or more" (p. 194). **A remembered darkness "four or five generations ago", recorded c. 1900-1960, may be 17th-century and should not be read as 1808 without a tephra date.**

### 7.4 Leads for 1808-09 from the whole book (not yet followed)
1. **Post-Tibito tephra in the upper Wage basin (Enga), site 400** (pp. 28, 88-91, 93-95). A 2.5 cm yellow-brown tephra lies above Tibito, at 25-50 cm depth, and is chemically distinct (Zr 274, Y 40 ppm). "neither the source of this upper tephra nor its age is known, other than that it is clearly younger than Tibito"; it is not from Doma Peaks; Blong suggests the Schouten Islands or Manam. He calls it "an enigma" that "requires further investigation". The local Waka-Enga legend (059, Wohlt) is the most severe on record: stones "like marbles" broke the roofs, "all birds and animals died", "Allegedly only three survivors". Its genealogical date is 1775-1825 or earlier (Table 30). **Inference:** a north-coast eruption after c. 1660 reached Enga; nothing ties it to 1808, but it is the only physical trace in the book of a younger PNG ash fall. Next step: look for later work on this tephra (Wohlt; Blong et al. 2018; Enga tephrostratigraphy).
2. **Manus darkness legend** (Carey 1938, p. 69). Widespread in the Manus group: the sun "not seen for many days, nor any light, and everything was covered by a thick layer of ashes"; possible source Baluan Island; "perhaps a hundred years or more" before 1937, so c. 1837 or earlier. Blong excluded it, since it is off the Tibito axis. **Inference:** the age is too vague to place, but it is a separate event worth one check: Carey's 1938 paper and any Manus ethnography (Mead, Fortune) that mentions the ash.
3. **Two times of darkness at Madang** (Mennis 1978, p. 199). The earlier one is tied to the disappearance of **Yomba Island**, presumed to be a Bismarck Sea volcano that collapsed. The Madang informants 066 (Bilbil) and 073 (Kranket, Mennis) describe ash and unusual waves. The later darkness is presumably Long Island. Not dated; worth reading Mennis's Madang work.
4. **Outliers Blong set aside:** Goodenough Island ash falls (Young 1971 p. 139); Saibai and Duan, Torres Strait (Laade); Bine near Daru; Bogia coast 078, which Z'Graggen tied to Manam 1877; Wanuma 094, Washkuk 098 and Wewak 099, which could be "almost any north coast volcano" (p. 95). Each is a separate small tradition with no date.
5. **Bismarck Sea grey-green sand** (Krause 1965, Bull. Geol. Soc. Am. 76: 27-42): a widespread sand layer in sea-floor samples. Marine cores are where an unrecorded 1808 Bismarck eruption could show; not followed.
6. **Doma Peaks** was once dated "90 to 400 years" before 1971 (Taylor 1971), from the Huli legend alone; Blong found no tephra younger than Tomba from it (p. 35). Ruled out.
7. **Long Island oral versions** (App. 3): no date beyond "at least two generations" before the informant's father; tidal wave "only once". Blong rules out any 19th-century date from mariners' sightings and Miklouho-Maclay (p. 194). Consistent with Part 6: Long Island is not our eruption.

**What this adds to the search.** For 1808-09 the book does two things. It gives numbers for testing any distant report: one to three days of booms, ash about a day per 1000 km later, and darkness lasting about one hour per 4 mm of ash. And it shows that in Melanesia a large eruption can survive only as a legend whose genealogical date is unreliable by up to two centuries. Of its leads, only the Wage post-Tibito tephra is physical evidence of a younger ash fall, and it has no date.

## Part 8. How a veil shows (and does not show) in a long twilight record: the 1888 Royal Society list (added 26 Oct 2026)

Source: Russell & Archibald, "Previous analogous glow phenomena", in Symons (ed.), *The Eruption of Krakatoa* (1888), pp. 384-403; Howard, *Climate of London* 2nd ed. vol. 2 (1833). **OCR only.** Details: notes/krakatoa_report_historical_twilights_2026-10-26.md, register B44.

- **1808-10 entries:** London (Plaistow, Luke Howard) only. 6 May 1808 "the twilight at Plaistow, at 9 p.m., was brilliant in the highest degree"; 27 Mar 1809 "lunar halo and coloured cirrus"; 1-2 Apr 1809 "an extensive blush, on the evening twilight, of rose-coloured red haze". Nothing for Nov 1808 - Mar 1809. Howard's 1810 total: 4 coloured-twilight days, the lowest of 1810-1818.
- **Marker lesson 1:** period observers wrote veil effects as "blush on the twilight", "rose-coloured haze", "luminous twilight", "coloured cirri", "sun and moon red on the horizon", "diverging shadows", "brilliant twilight". These are useful search terms, but most such notes are ordinary cloud colour: Russell & Archibald admit "many of the observations refer simply to cirri or cirro-cumuli coloured red by the setting sun".
- **Marker lesson 2:** one keen observer's daily diary is not a calibrated instrument. Howard's counts rise in 1811 (33 days) when a second note-taker ("T. F.") joins and an Azores eruption draws attention, not when the 1809 aerosol was in the air. So a run of coloured skies needs other signs with it (dim or rayless sun, pale or blue sun, a halo or Bishop's ring, a long-lasting twilight, a dark lunar eclipse) before it can count as a veil.
- **Marker lesson 3:** the 1888 compilers paired English twilights with any eruption a few days earlier (Azores 1808 and 1811, St Vincent 1812). Such pairings are their hypothesis, not evidence.
