## 1. Result in brief

1. **Only one trace was printed as news at the time it happened**: Caldas's "Noticia meteorológica" in the *Semanario del Nuevo Reyno de Granada* (5 Feb 1809). The weekly was a privately run scientific paper. It was printed "Con lic. del Sup. Gob." (by licence of the Superior Government, i.e. the viceroy), and its subscriber list in the same issue opens with "El Exmo. Sr. Virey del Reyno, por 7. exemplares" [page image]. The Noticia was written to *calm* people. Caldas says "el vulgo sencillo lo ha tomado como indicio seguro de grandes calamidades. ¡Quántos me han consultado y á quantos he tenido que serenar!" [page image]. He closes by noting that after the darkened sun of 1673 "las cosas naturales y políticas se mantuvieron en el estado que exigían las circunstancias" [page image]. That is a reassurance with a political edge, written five months after the Spanish crisis of 1808 had reached Santafé [background]. The issue before it calls the war "la mas exterminadora del comercio" [page image]. Section 3.1 has the full passages.
2. **Every other trace comes from an institution's working record or from a later book**, not from the press:
   - a fur-trade company's post journal (HBC, York Factory);
   - logs kept for the Royal Navy, the East India Company and the Post Office packet service (Penelope, Tigris, Townshend, EIC fleet);
   - colonial edicts and an auction sheet from Daendels's Batavia;
   - court and domain chronicles (Nguyễn Veritable Records, Qing county gazetteers, Tottori domain annals);
   - an Edo scholar's private chronicle of shogunal politics;
   - parish burial books, a Dutch trading post's register and a Spanish ship's charts;
   - two diaries (Bentley in Salem, Howard in London);
   - three books printed years later: Unanue (Madrid 1815), Raffles (London 1817) and Acosta (Paris 1849, printing Pérez Valencia's register).
3. **War shapes almost every record.** It decided which ships sailed and in what company, and what filled the pages around the sky entries. Examples:
   - The Edo red-sky entry sits two entries after the shogunate punished the Saga lord over the *Phaeton* incident at Nagasaki, and one entry after rewards for service on the Ezo frontier [page image].
   - The *Tigris* "hazy" days were logged in a homeward East India Company convoy. That convoy had lost three ships in a hurricane four weeks earlier [text; computed].
   - The *Townshend* was a Post Office mail packet on the Rio de Janeiro run. The Portuguese court had moved to Rio that year [background].
   - The Batavia rice edicts sit among orders for forced labour [page image, earlier notes].
4. **What this means for weighing the traces [Claude's inference].**
   - Most of these records were not made to notice the sky. A sky remark in them is therefore unusual for that writer, which is what gives McNab's "darkest day" and Katayama's red sky some weight.
   - For the same reason, their silences are weak evidence.
   - The one public trace (Caldas) was shaped by a need to calm a worried public. That pushes the text towards playing the event down, not up.
   - Fair copies sent to head offices can drop the sky remarks altogether: the HBC London copy omits "the darkest day".

**The passages that show the social reception (p. 39).**
> "Algunos han creido que este fenómeno es único, extraordinario y casi fuera de las leyes comunes de la naturaleza, y el vulgo sencillo lo ha tomado como indicio seguro de grandes calamidades. ¡Quántos me han consultado y á quantos he tenido que serenar! English: "Some have believed this phenomenon unique, extraordinary and almost outside the common laws of nature, and simple people have taken it as a sure sign of great calamities. How many have consulted me, and how many I have had to calm! The peace of mind of everyone, over a thing that has nothing extraordinary about it ... has obliged me to fill two pages of our Semanario ... Hence the languid, silver-coloured Sun ... the cold, the frosts and everything that makes up the phenomenon that has alarmed weak spirits."

pp. 39-40, on the darkened sun of 1673:
> "En el Reynado de Felipe IV. en todo el año de 1673 el Sol se vió en Colonia, en Ulma, en Hidelberg y en toda la Europa obscurecido y de color de ceniza. Los Astrologos de aquella edad, es decir, los profetas fanaticos de la suerte del genero humano, anunciaron grandes cosas; el vulgo y los ignorantes temieron; los años pasaron; las cosas naturales y políticas se mantuvieron en el estado que exigian las circunstancias; el tiempo desengañó á los preocupados y manifestó que la obscuridad del Sol no era otra cosa que un meteoro que no tenia mas de extraordinario que el ser raro."

English: "... The astrologers of that age, that is, the fanatical prophets of mankind's fate, announced great things; the common people and the ignorant were afraid; the years passed; natural and **political** affairs remained in the state that circumstances required; time undeceived the prejudiced ..."

(Note: Felipe IV died in 1665, so "Reynado de Felipe IV ... 1673" is Caldas's slip [Claude's observation].)

The Núm. 4 context (29 Jan 1809, p. 25), on the Veracruz trade tables supplied by the Cartagena administrator Ignacio Cavero:
> "llegan hasta la mitad de 1807; pero con los claros y vacios que ha ocasionado una guerra la mas exterminadora del comercio: por lo que se ha juzgado inutil y aun vicioso traerlos à monto ..."

**Historical context note [background, not checked in this pass].** News of the Bayonne abdications (May 1808) and of the Spanish juntas reached New Granada in the second half of 1808. Santafé swore allegiance to Ferdinand VII and recognised the Junta Central. The *Semanario* of 1809 also printed Junta Central notices (Núm. 26, B-section notes). A year later, in August 1809, the Quito junta formed. Camilo Torres's *Representación del Cabildo* (the "Memorial de agravios") is dated November 1809. In January 1809 the city was therefore waiting for news from a Spain at war, with a viceroy in office and a learned public that later split over self-rule.

**[Claude's inference]** Read against that setting, Caldas's closing sentence is more than science writing. He tells his readers, the viceroy among them, that sky omens do not foretell political upheaval. This also explains why the Noticia played the event down ("nada tiene de extraordinario") while describing it as unprecedented in his own observations (F15). For the evidence, the reassurance is useful: it shows that many people in Santafé saw the veil, talked about it and feared it. Caldas did not see it alone through a telescope.

**[Claude's inference]** The chronicle is about the shogunate: appointments, marriages, rewards, punishments. The red-sky entry is wedged between two frontier-defence items, Nagasaki (Phaeton) and Ezo (the Russian raids of 1806-07). Earlier in the same pages are rice dearness and an epidemic. A chronicler of politics thought the sky worth a line in this company, which supports reading it as conspicuous. Nothing on these pages suggests an omen reading of it; the entry is plain description.

## 4. Patterns across the traces [Claude's inference]

1. **Institutions decide what was kept.** Company (HBC, EIC), navy, Post Office, colonial government, court and church records survive because institutions archived them. What reached the head office was edited: the HBC London copy drops the sky remark. The original post or ship copies are therefore the sensitive layer, and they are mostly the ones still unread (ship remarks columns, post journals, fleet-mates' logs).
2. **War is the frame on almost every page.** It shaped the paper's money, the convoy's route, the Dutch post's worries and the shogun's agenda. This helps explain the silence. Newspapers in 1808-09 were filled with the Peninsular War (Gaceta de Madrid, Gaceta de Caracas and Gaceta de México were read for this project with negative results), and natural news had little room.
3. **Only Bogotá shows a public reaction.** Caldas's text shows fear, consultation and talk of calamities. No other trace shows how people in general responded to the sky. Records that might show a public reaction elsewhere (sermons, rogativas, cabildo minutes, temple and shrine records, Balinese babad) are where social evidence of a conspicuous veil could still be found. U158 (Redactor Americano, for rogativas in Bogotá) is the nearest such lead.
4. **The voices of the people who lived nearest the candidate volcanoes are not in this set.** Every trace above was written by a European, creole, Japanese, Chinese or Vietnamese official, scholar or crew. The Indigenous and local histories of Melanesia, the Moluccas and the southern Philippines reach this project only through oral-history work (melanesia_oral strand) and later colonial hearsay (F13). The documentary record is a record of empires at war, not a map of who could have seen the eruption.
