# Krakatoa report (1888), historical list of coloured twilights: entries 1805-1812

Thread "Coloured twilights in the Krakatoa report", 26 Oct 2026. Register section B44.

**Result in one line:** the Royal Society report's historical list has **no** coloured twilight, dry fog, blue or dim sun anywhere in the world between 6 May 1808 and 27 March 1809. Its only 1809 entry (London, 27 Mar and 1-2 Apr 1809) is Luke Howard's, which we already had. Its 1811 "coloured skies" come from the same London observers and the authors tie them to the Azores eruptions of 1811. Howard's own day counts show only **4** coloured-twilight days in 1810 against **33** in 1811, so the 1811 skies do not look like a late phase of the 1809 veil (inference, with caveats in section 4).

### 2b. The discussion that follows the list (pp. 402-403, leaves 518-519, OCR)

- "The number of days in the years, 1810 to 1820, inclusive, on which red sunsets, luminous twilights, &c., were noted by Howard, are as follows": **1810: 4; 1811: 33; 1812: 17; 1813: 31; 1814: 21; 1815: 24; 1816: 5; 1817: 18; 1818: 23; 1819: 2; 1820: 2.** Footnote: "Howard's observations are more detailed for this than for any other period."
- "These figures, therefore, do not show any great preponderance of twilight phenomena in the years of eruptions. But many of the observations refer simply to cirri or cirro-cumuli coloured red by the setting sun".
- "If we take only those years in which the sky itself is stated to have been much coloured, or in which there was a succession of highly-coloured sunsets, or in which the redness is stated to have lasted long, we find them to be the following: 1811, 1812, 1813, 1814, 1815, 1818."
- "It will be noticed that in the three instances of eruptions at the Azores, a bright or coloured twilight was observed in England within a week." (The three are 1 May 1808 → 6 May 1808; 31 Jan 1811 → 3 Feb 1811; 13 Jun 1811 → 16 Jun 1811.)
- p. 452 (leaf 568), in the answer to objections: "in the period from 1600 to 1883, the following years were distinguished both for great eruptions and for extraordinary sky phenomena: — 1636, 1680, 1721, 1755, 1783, 1811, 1812, 1813, 1814, 1815, 1818, 1831, 1845, 1846, 1872." **1808, 1809 and 1810 are not among them.**

## 4. Are the 1811 twilights the late phase of the 1809 veil? (Claude's assessment, inference)

**Probably not.**
1. **Wrong shape in time.** A veil from an eruption in late 1808 should give the most twilights in 1809 and fewer in 1810, then fade by 1811. Howard's counts run the other way: 1810 has only 4 days, the lowest of 1810-1818, and 1811 has 33. The 1809 sulfate reached the Greenland surface in Aug-Oct 1809, with its deposition midpoint at Summit in Sep 1810 (Cole-Dai 2009), so most of the aerosol was already out of the stratosphere during 1810, when London saw almost nothing.
2. **The report's own explanation fits the dates.** The 1811 coloured skies start on 16 June, three days after the Sabrina eruption began on 13 June 1811, and the Feb 1811 ones three days after the 31 Jan 1811 Azores eruption. Howard printed the Sabrina account next to the June notes. A small Azores eruption is unlikely to colour London skies for six months, though, so Sabrina is a trigger for attention more than a full cause.
3. **The record itself changed.** In 1811 Howard's book adds notes signed "T. F." (probably Thomas Forster, author of *Researches about Atmospheric Phaenomena*, 1813, a keen recorder of sunset colours). Russell & Archibald admit that "many of the observations refer simply to cirri or cirro-cumuli coloured red by the setting sun" and that the counts "do not show any great preponderance of twilight phenomena in the years of eruptions". More observers and more attention can produce more coloured days without any change in the sky.
4. **Something else is possible.** A separate, unidentified eruption in 1811 or a small 1810-1812 aerosol load could also be behind the 1811 skies, but nothing found here points to one, and testing it is outside this project's window.

**Caveat:** this rests on one London record of uneven authorship, and counting "coloured" days is a weak measure. The absence of 1810 twilights is a **negative** that fits the ice-core timing; it is not evidence about where the eruption was.
