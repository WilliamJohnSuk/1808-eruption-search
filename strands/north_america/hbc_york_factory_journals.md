## Headline
**B.239/a/119, PDF p8L (ms p.4): "4 Friday The darkest day I have seen in Hudsons Bay"**. This is the entry for **Friday 4 November 1808** (the weekday has been checked against the calendar). It was read at 400 dpi and **the reading is certain**. No cause is given: there is no mention of snow, gale, rain or smoke that day or the days either side. Nov 3: "Ind[ian]s went away". Nov 5: "Three wooders came home". The London copy (B.239/a/115) summarises this week and **omits the remark**. It is the only spontaneous remark about darkness or the sky found in about two and a half years of these journals.
- **Interpretation.** It cannot tell a heavy overcast or snow-laden sky in early November (low sun, about 6° at noon on the solstice side) apart from smoke or an aerosol veil. A writer who already had a previous full season at York (he was writing B.239/a/114 in 1807–08) calling it "the darkest day I have seen" does mark it as exceptional. Late-season forest-fire smoke is possible but unlikely after freeze-up began, since river ice had been running since 5 Oct. **Recommended cross-check:** the Churchill (B.42/a), Severn (B.198/a), Albany (B.3/a), Moose (B.135/a) and inland-post journals for 3–5 Nov 1808, and any Canadian or New England "dark day" reports for that date.

## How the writers record weather (sensitivity of a negative)
- **None of the volumes has weather columns.** There is no thermometer, wind table or sky register. Weather appears as a passing clause in perhaps 10–25% of daily entries, and in almost none of the weekly summaries.
- **McNab's vocabulary (1807–09):** "Snow at times", "Snow & drift", "as in Decr/Jany", "Mild weather", "weather uncommonly fine", "Weather fine as in July", "weather remarkably mild", "Weather grows severe", "Cold weather", "Sharp weather", "hard frost", "heavy rain", "Blows a gale", "thick snowy weather". **Cook's vocabulary (1809–11):** "Weather mild", "Weather remarkably mild", "Weather intensely cold", "Rainy bad weather", "Bad weather", "Weather too bad for outdoor work", "Weather more violent than yesterday", "Weather too violent for Ind[ian]s to depart".
- **No entry in any volume read uses** haze, hazy, smoke, smoky, dry fog, or "thick" in the sense of haze. Nor does any entry describe the sun's or moon's colour or brightness, halos, coronas, twilight or sunset colours, aurora, booms or reports, or dust on the snow. **Aurora is never mentioned, even though it is common at York.** This shows the journals do not record optical phenomena. **A negative is therefore of low sensitivity**, and the single "darkest day" remark is notable precisely because such remarks are absent otherwise.

## Month-by-month character, Sept 1808 – mid-1810 (York Factory)
- **Sept 1808:** Stormy and overcast: "the first fine day for weeks past" on 28 Sept. Snow from 16 Sept, and a "tremendous Gale … Snow & drift as in Decr" on 24 Sept. No sky or optical remarks.
- **Oct 1808:** Hard frost and shore ice from 3–5 Oct, ice driving in the river, and the **river fast above the factory by 13 Oct [location uncertain]**, roughly a month earlier than the 10 Nov 1807 freeze-up. Inland posts later reported an "early winter" in autumn 1808 (Oxford House, Swan River).
- **Nov 1808:** **4 Nov, "The darkest day I have seen in Hudsons Bay"**, with no cause stated. Otherwise only routine entries; foxes unusually numerous.
- **Dec 1808 – Jan 1809:** Daily entries with **no weather remarks at all**. 115 weekly summaries are likewise silent.
- **Feb 1809:** "Weather very severe" (24 Feb). Indians complain of the "excessive severity of the weather". No snow for "several weeks" before 3 Mar, so a cold and dry month.
- **Mar 1809:** Very cold on 1 Mar; snow and drift on 3 Mar; "fine" and "soft weather" on 20–22 Mar. Partridges absent.
- **Apr 1809:** First goose on 21 Apr (normal). Cold setback on 28–30 Apr, with "Snow & Drift as in Decr" on 30 Apr. Famine among northern Indians.
- **May 1809:** Snow and drift from 1–6 May ("as in Decr"), heavy snow on 20 May. Breakup on 18 May (normal). Fine weather on 16–17 May. The Indians "never saw so few" geese (24 May), though 1808 was also poor. A cold spring at the coast, but not an extreme one.
- **June – Aug 1809:** Inland vegetation was ahead of 1808 (4 June). Routine gales and rain. High water in the river (14 July). No remarks about the sky.
- **Sept 1809:** Snow and hard frost on 6–7 Sept. Change of writer (W. H. Cook takes over on 16 Sept).
- **Oct 1809:** Ice and snow from 14–15 Oct, remarkably mild with the river clear on 19 Oct, intensely cold on 24 Oct, then storms. Freeze-up was later than in 1808.
- **Nov 1809 – Mar 1810:** Violent storms on 16–18 Nov. Few weather remarks after that. Extreme hare and ptarmigan scarcity, starvation complaints, and "unpropitious seasons".
- **Apr – June 1810:** Storms on 5–6 and 23 Apr. **Late spring**: first goose on 9 May (about 18 days later than 1809), breakup on 19–20 May with an ice-jam flood, and ice still being cleared from the bank on 2 June.
- **No month** contains any report of haze, smoky or dim sun, coloured twilights, halos, aurora, ash or booms.
