### 3. David Thompson
**(a) *David Thompson's Narrative* (ed. J. B. Tyrrell, Champlain Society 1916)**
- IA: https://archive.org/details/davidthompsonsn00tyrrgoog (server ia601603; OCR search).
- Kootanae House, **22 Dec 1808** (scan pp. 523–524; Tyrrell's chapter heading includes "Mock Sun").
  - Verbatim OCR: "At 8½ am the Sun was clear, and the sky clear to the left of the Sun … but to the right a dense atmosphere about twenty degrees from the Sun". Continuing: "In this a very bright halo was formed, at times it had the colours of the Rainbow, but of a deeper tint." About 9 am the halo "formed a mock sun fully equal in splendor to the real Sun"; his men said there were two suns. He adds "… but never so perfect a mock Sun."
  - **Interpretation:** this is a classic 22° parhelion (sun dog) in ice-crystal cirrus or diamond dust. The "dense atmosphere" sits at about 20°, the halo radius, so it is the ice-crystal patch itself. It is ordinary winter optics. The date (22 Dec 1808) does fall in the window, but nothing marks it as volcanic: there is no general haze and no dimmed sun ("the Sun was clear"). **Not diagnostic.** Verification: OCR snippets only.
- Scan p. 552, weather register: "April 18. 5 am +38 Clear 2 pm +71 hazy. 9 PM +38 calm." The year is ambiguous from OCR context (Apr 1809 Kootanae House or Apr 1810 Saleesh House); context leans to 1810. An afternoon spring haze at +71 °F is not diagnostic; dust or smoke are likely.

### 9. Daniel Drake, *Natural and Statistical View, or Picture of Cincinnati and the Miami Country* (Cincinnati 1815): Ohio Valley, interior
- IA: https://archive.org/details/naturalstatistic00drak (OCR search). Drake kept weather notes from about 1806. "Since the beginning of the year 1809, a register of the winds has been kept at this place" (scan p. 104).
- **Scan p. 114:** "The earliest autu[m]nal frost observed in the vicinity of this place, was on the 9th of August 1809." He adds: "In general, the last of September is the earliest period at which white frost is perceptible in the valley of the Ohio."
  - **This is a record-early frost at Cincinnati (39°N) in summer 1809**, about 7 weeks before normal. It is consistent with a cool NH summer in 1809.
  - Single-station frosts in August can come from local radiative cooling in a valley, so this needs corroboration. Event 9 Aug 1809; written c. 1814; published 1815. Independent of the South American accounts.
- **Scan p. 96 (spring 1810):** "Even during the same spring, the progress of vegetation is occasionally suspended. This was the case, to a remarkable degree, in 1810. By the 22d & 25th February, the buds of the water maple and weeping willow began to open; by the 5th of March they became stationary … continued without advancing till near the 10th of April. On the 15th of that month, the forest around Cincinnati exhibited … not a single leaf". This was a cold March – early April 1810 following an early-warm February.
- **Scan p. 116:** a generic description of the autumn "Indian summer": "the atmosphere is, for the most part, dry, serene and smoky, through which the sun and moon exhibit in the morning and evening a face of darkened crimson". This is a normal annual autumn haze (biomass smoke), not specific to any year. It is a useful caution: in interior North America, a "smoky" sky with a crimson sun in Oct–Nov is the normal Indian-summer regime.
- Scan p. 247: smoky and hazy days before the 16 Dec 1811 New Madrid earthquake. Outside the window.
