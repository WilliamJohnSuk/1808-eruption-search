This wind was followed on the coast by scarcity of water, coughs and catarrhs, which lasted until the month of December. In the middle of it [December] there began to appear to the SW, between the hill of Chorrillos and the sea, at sunset, an evening twilight that lit up the atmosphere: it shot up from the horizon N.S. Reading = mid-Dec 1808 → mid-Feb 1809 is the natural one, but strictly it is an inference (one could argue a later December, though nothing suggests that). Footnote written after Feb 1809 and before printing (1815); the same p. 14 carries another note citing observations made "En 1812" by Samuel Curson, so revisions to these notes extend to ≥1812.
- **Observation location**: Unanue writes from Lima ("esta Capital"); the glow was seen **toward the SW, over the gap between the Morro/cerro de Chorrillos and the sea** — i.e. Chorrillos is the *direction* of the sky phenomenon as seen from Lima, NOT the observer's location. ### 1e. Interpretation / alternatives for the Unanue glow
- Supports an unusual, persistent evening glow ~mid-Dec 1808 → mid-Feb 1809, consistent in timing with Caldas (Bogotá veil from 11 Dec 1808). Unanue is independent of Caldas (no reference to him).
- **Alternative: zodiacal light.** A cone rising from the western horizon toward the zenith (the ecliptic is near-vertical at tropical latitudes), persisting to ~20:00, is a classic zodiacal-light description, and Lima's summer (Dec–Apr) is the clear-sky season after the winter garúa/stratus, so an ordinary annual phenomenon could become newly noticeable. My own computation: Lima sunset ≈18:20 local mean time (mid-Dec) and ≈18:30 LMT (mid-Feb); astronomical twilight ends ≈1 h 15–20 min after sunset at 12°S, i.e. ≈19:35–19:50. "Hasta las ocho" (≈1 h 40 min after sunset) therefore runs *past* the end of astronomical twilight, which is long for a purple-light/afterglow (these usually fade by solar depression ~10–12°, ≈45–60 min after sunset) unless the clock time is loose. Points against zodiacal light: "todas las noches" for two months (my mean-phase computation: new moons ≈17 Dec 1808 and ≈15 Feb 1809, full moon ≈1 Jan and ≈31 Jan 1809 — moonlit evenings around first quarter/full moon would wash out zodiacal light), Unanue (a long-time Lima observer and cosmógrafo) treats it as unprecedented, and the start/end both "a mediados" coincide with new moons only (no monthly modulation reported). Net: consistent with an aerosol twilight glow, but zodiacal light is a genuine alternative that must be flagged. Neither reading is fixed by the text.
- The 30 Apr 1808 "polvillo colorado hediondo" on a violent S wind is a separate, earlier event (dust storm from the coastal desert/Paracas-type "paracas" wind; "hediondo" could hint at sulphurous/ash material but no volcano is named). It predates the proposed Dec 1808 eruption by 7 months; not evidence for it, but worth noting as a local aerosol event.
