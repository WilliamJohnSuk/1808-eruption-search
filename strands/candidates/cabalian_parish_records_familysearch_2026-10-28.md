## 3. Who kept the books in 1808 (background, for reading the hands)

Leyte and Samar were Jesuit missions from 1595 until the expulsion of 1768, then passed to the Augustinians; the Augustinians, short of men, ceded Guiuan to the Franciscans in 1795 (Franciscan pastor from 1804) and the rest of their Leyte parishes in 1834, the north-east to the Franciscans and the west and south to the secular clergy of Cebu [text: Wikipedia "Archdiocese of Palo"; Guiuan parish history essay, msgrlope.blogspot.com]. So the Cabalían priest of 1808-10 was an Augustinian or a secular priest of the Diocese of Cebu, and the Guiuan priest a Franciscan; the Bohol parishes were Augustinian Recollect [memory]. Entries will be in Spanish, with the older formula in a mix of Spanish and Latin; a Visayan-speaking Filipino secular priest's hand is possible in Leyte [memory].

## 4. Why the regional books are a real test (reasoning; suggestion)

- Size. The 1809 event put roughly half of Tambora's sulfur into the stratosphere (B46), which needs a VEI 6 or a large VEI 5. Eruptions of that class drop measurable ash hundreds of kilometres downwind: Pinatubo (VEI 6, June 1991) laid centimetres of ash on Manila 90 km away and traces across the South China Sea; Tambora's ash fell on Java and Borneo [memory, figures approximate].
- Wind. From November to February the surface and low-tropospheric flow over the central Philippines is the north-east monsoon, from the north-east and east, so low ash from a Cabalían eruption in our date window (mid-November to early December 1808) would travel west-south-west over Sogod Bay, Bohol, southern Cebu and Negros. The upper-level winds at 10°N in that season are more variable, and a stratospheric plume could spread either way; the Guiuan book covers the north-east sector in case of that, or in case the eruption was mid-year after all (which the calibrated deposition band of B62 would need for Cabalían) [memory for the climatology; nothing verified for 1808].
- Priests noticed. Parish priests and friars in the Philippines wrote accounts of Taal 1754 and Mayon 1814 and entered calamities in their books; the Visayan parish registers of this period often carry year-end notes and "nota" entries about epidemics, Moro raids and storms [memory]. A plinian eruption 100-170 km away would also have been heard (Tambora was heard at 1,800 km; Krakatau at 4,800 km [memory]) and would have darkened the sky for a day. Sulfur-rich ash and a failed harvest tend to show as a burial rise over the following six to twelve months.
- What silence would mean. No note and no burial rise in Guiuan plus two or three Bohol parishes, for November 1808 to December 1809, would make a VEI 6 at Cabalían hard to believe; it would not exclude a small eruption, but a small eruption cannot be the 1809 event. This complements, from the ground, the latitude-band argument of B62 (section 2.1), which already placed Cabalían outside the band for a November-December eruption.
- What has already been read. The Manila side is thin but not empty: the project's Spanish-language sweep (Gazeta de Madrid, Gazeta de México, Diario de México and the rest, register sections B12-B34) found no Manila report of an eruption, darkness or ashfall in 1808-10, and the Asian strands (B48, B51) found nothing in the printed Philippine chronicles. Those are weak negatives; a parish register 100 km from the vent is a stronger one.

Weak, because a smaller eruption, a fallout carried west or south, or priests who wrote no remarks would all leave the same silence; it says nothing about the distant candidates (B53).
