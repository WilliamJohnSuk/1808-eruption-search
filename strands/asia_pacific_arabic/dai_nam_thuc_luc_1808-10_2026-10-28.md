## 2. Calendar conversion [calc]

Month-start dates are from the Chinese (Qing Shixian) lunar calendar, computed with the `sxtwl` library and cross-checked against astronomical new moons (`ephem`): every month start in 1808-11 falls on the civil day of the new moon at the 107°E meridian. Sexagenary day names printed in the text were converted with the same tool and agree with the month table at 30 points (for example Mậu thìn = 21 Feb 1809, 8th day of the 1st month; Quý hợi = 11 Feb 1810, 8th day of the 1st month; Ất mão = 4 Apr 1810, 1st day of the 3rd month, the day of the solar eclipse the chronicle records, section 3). Where the text gives only a month, the date range of that month is given.

Two calendar facts in the chronicle itself [page image]:

- **Vietnam had a leap 6th month in 1808, China a leap 5th.** Quyển XXXV runs "Tháng 5" (p. 726), "Tháng 6" (p. 730), "Tháng 6 nhuận" (p. 731), then "mùa thu, tháng 7, ngày mồng 1" (p. 733). The Qing calendar has 閏五月 in 1808. Both calendars share the same new moons, so the Vietnamese 6th month = 24 Jun-22 Jul 1808 and the Vietnamese leap 6th = 23 Jul-21 Aug 1808; months 7-12 and all of 1809-10 coincide [calc]. The difference is what the mean-solar-term rule of the Ming Datong method gives: the mean Dashu term falls on 22 Jul 1808, inside the 24 Jun-22 Jul month, while the true term (used by the Qing since 1645) falls on 23 Jul [calc].
- **The chronicle says why.** Gia Long 9, 4th month (May 1810), p. 785: Nguyễn Hữu Thận, back from the Qing embassy, presents the *Đại Thanh lịch tượng khảo thành* and reports "Lịch vạn toàn của nước ta cùng với sách Đại Thanh thời hiến, từ trước đều dùng phép lịch Đại thống của nhà Minh, hơn ba trăm năm, chưa có sửa đổi, càng lâu càng sai. Khoảng năm Khang Hy nhà Thanh mới tham hợp phép lịch của Tây dương mà chép thành sách này" (our Vạn Toàn calendar and the Qing Shixian both formerly used the Ming Datong method, unrevised for 300 years and ever more in error; in Kangxi's reign the Qing adopted the Western method). The Khâm thiên giám was ordered to learn it. So the Nguyễn calendar of 1808-10 was still the Datong-based Vạn Toàn calendar; month starts can in principle differ from the Qing by a day when a new moon falls near midnight, which the eclipse date and the thirty sexagenary checks show did not happen for any dated entry here.

## 5. Assessment (suggestions, labelled)

1. **No optical sign of the veil at 11-21°N, Nov 1808 to 1810 [page image, negative].** The chronicle records one sky event in three years, the eclipse of 4 Apr 1810, and nothing on the sun, moon or sky colour. This is a weak negative: the Đệ nhất kỷ is terse on omens (no earthquake in three years, no lunar eclipse), it was compiled in 1821-48 from palace records, and the Vietnamese literary tradition of this reign, like the Japanese one (B43), had no reason to connect a dim sun with a distant volcano. It is nevertheless the first negative from mainland South-East Asia at these latitudes, and it adds the Nguyễn court to the list of courts (Qing, Chosŏn, Ryukyu, Nguyễn) that noted nothing in the winter of 1808/09.

2. **A 1809 drought in the Red River delta [page image], not previously in our files.** The edict of Jan 1810 (p. 771) says the 1809 autumn crop in the five inner trấn was lost "nơi thì nắng hạn lâu ngày, nơi thì gió bão nước mặn" (long drought in some places, typhoon and salt water in others), and the Feb 1810 entry (p. 772) calls the 1809 disasters in Kinh Bắc, Hải Dương, Yên Quảng and Sơn Nam hạ "hạn và lụt" (drought and flood). With the 26 Guangdong-Guangxi drought sites of 1809 (REACHES, B60) and the Batavia "unusual drought of the season" of Oct 1809 (B51), the 1809 summer-monsoon season now shows drought at 6°S (Java), 21°N (Hanoi) and 22-24°N (Guangdong-Guangxi). Whether this is a volcanic monsoon response, an ENSO signal (B58: corals El Niño-like Dec 1808-May 1809, tree rings La Niña) or coincidence is not decidable from the chronicle [speculation]; the chronicle gives no cause.

3. **The Bắc Thành famine of 1809-10 had more than one cause [page image].** The same pages record the 1808 risings that devastated 358 villages in the four worst-hit trấn (p. 803), granary fraud, desertion and coin shortage. Officials themselves list "hạn và lụt" first, but the famine cannot be read as a clean climate signal.

4. **Drought in 1808 is pre-eruption and not diagnostic.** Quảng Đức, Bình Định and Phú Yên were in drought in May-June 1808 and Nghệ An was "a drought year" with hunger by June-July 1808, before any plausible eruption date. Central Vietnam's spring-summer dry spell is normal climatology there; the entries show the chronicle reports drought when it hurts the tax, which makes the 1809-10 northern entries more credible as a departure.

5. **What the court was collecting, and where it might survive [text, suggestion].** Three administrative orders in the window set up exactly the record a volcanic winter would leave: daily weather registers at every dinh and trấn (Feb-Mar 1808, p. 720), monthly reports of rice prices, farming and "nắng mưa" from every province (Sep-Oct 1808, p. 738), and a province-wide survey of planting and harvest weather (Sep-Oct 1809, p. 763). These reports, if any survive, would be among the châu bản (vermilion records) of the Gia Long reign at the National Archives Centre I, Hanoi; the Gia Long châu bản are known to be few. Listed as U88. Phan Thúc Trực's private *Quốc sử di biên* (1850s), which records omens and prices for 1802-47 that the official annals omit, is the obvious next Vietnamese text and is not online (U67).

6. **Control still to run (suggestion).** The same PDF covers Gia Long 14-15 (1815-16). Reading those pages for the Tambora year would show how this chronicle treats a veil we know reached Vietnam, and would turn the 1809 silence from "weak negative" into a calibrated one. About 50 scans; a few hours.
